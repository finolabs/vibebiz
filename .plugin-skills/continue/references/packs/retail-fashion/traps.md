# Diagnostic traps

Readings of fashion data that look right and are often wrong. For each: the trap, why it fools people, and the question that rules it out.

## Missing sizes read as weak demand
A style shows low sales, but the popular sizes ran out early or never arrived in some shops. Low sales then reflect missing stock, not low interest. Rule out: for the weak style, in what share of shop-weeks were the main sizes in stock? Did sales slow before or after the sizes ran out?

## Promotion uplift confused with seasonality or weather
Sales rose during a promotion, but the same weeks rise every year (Lunar New Year, back to school) or a cold snap sold coats. Crediting the promotion overstates its effect. Rule out: what did the same weeks do last year without a promotion, and what did shops or regions without the promotion do?

## Channel shift read as growth
The website grows while shop sales fall by a similar amount: customers moved, the total did not grow. The same holds for marketplace against own website. Rule out: did the total across all channels change, and did margin change with the shift?

## Pull-forward read as a gain
A promotion lifts one week and the weeks after drop, because customers bought early. Rule out: compare the whole period before and after, not the promotion week only.

## Average price up only because the mix changed
Average selling price rises when cheap items sell out, not because prices rose. Rule out: compare price per style, not the overall average.

## Sell-through of a young style compared with an old one
A new style at week 3 looks weak against a style at week 10. Rule out: compare at the same age.

## Conversion up because traffic fell
Conversion rises when low-intent visitors disappear (for example an advertising campaign stopped). Orders did not grow. Rule out: look at orders and visitors, not the ratio alone.

## Returns left out of sales
Online sales are often reported before returns; a size-heavy category can lose a large part of them. Rule out: ask for sales net of returns, same period.

## One star shop or style carries the average
A few shops or styles produce most of the result, and the idea is judged on the average. Rule out: ask for the spread across shops and styles, not only the total.

## Loyalty members read as all customers
Customers who give a phone number at checkout are often the best customers. Results from them do not describe the rest. Rule out: what share of receipts is linked to a customer?
