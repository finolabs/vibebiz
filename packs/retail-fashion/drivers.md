# Drivers of fashion retail results

Each driver says what it is, why it matters and the question it raises for an idea. Use it to find where a problem really sits before sketching a solution.

## Newness and drop cadence
How often new styles reach shops or the website, and how big each drop is. A fast cadence gives repeat visitors a reason to return but raises the number of styles to buy, price and stock. A slow cadence lowers the reason to revisit. Ask: how often do customers see something new, and what share of sales comes from styles under eight weeks old?

## Sell-through
The share of bought units already sold. It is the central health sign of a style or a season: low means too much was bought or the style is wrong; very high may mean stock-outs and lost sales. See `metrics.md`.

## Size curve
The spread of sizes bought against the spread customers actually want. A style can sell well and still leave unsellable stock if the middle sizes run out first and the extremes stay. Ask: which sizes sell out first, and who decides how many of each size go to each shop?

## Markdown depth and timing
How much and how early prices are cut. Early shallow cuts protect margin; late deep cuts clear stock at a loss and teach customers to wait. Ask: in which week of a style's life does the first cut happen, and by how much?

## Seasonality
Demand follows the calendar: Lunar New Year (new outfits, usually the largest peak in Vietnam and neighbouring markets), back to school, mid-season sales, year-end sales, and weather turns (cool season, rainy season). Ask: which weeks carry the most revenue, and does the plan compare like weeks with like weeks across years?

## Returns driven by size and fit
Online fashion returns are mostly about size and fit, not defects. Each return costs shipping both ways, handling, and sometimes an item that cannot be resold. Ask: what share of returns says "wrong size" or "does not fit"?

## Omnichannel fulfilment
Stock may sit in shops, a warehouse or both. Pick-up in shop, shipping from shop and transfers between shops decide whether a sale is possible when one place runs out. Ask: can an online order draw on shop stock, and who owns that stock in the books?

## Cash flow and open-to-buy
A fashion season is bought months ahead. Money stays tied up in stock until it sells. Ask: how much buying budget is left for the rest of the season, and what happens when sales fall short?

## Channel and customer mix
Shops, marketplaces, the own website and chat-app sales behave differently in price, margin and customer data. Ask: which channel is the idea about, and does it move sales from another channel rather than add new ones?
